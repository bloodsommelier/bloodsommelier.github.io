Blood Barn (2025)

Or 76 Minutes of Life Given Over to Waste

Perhaps the bitterest form of homage is one so devoted that it dispenses with the inconvenience of having an idea of its own. Blood Barn (2025) knows the codes of ’80s horror, reproduces its mannerisms with remarkable diligence, and then considers the job done.

Directed and co-written by Gabriel Bernini alongside Alexandra Jade, this small, pretentious independent film recycles the conventional premise: Josie (Lena Redford) and her friends travel to her family’s rural property in search of a few days of rest and fun. Naturally, as the rules demand, the place has a sinister past and lies under the malignant influence of the protagonist’s ancestors. From there, the screenplay does little more than string together supernatural manifestations and the grand philosophical proposition of “every man for himself,” with The Evil Dead as its most obvious point of reference.

There is nothing wrong with starting a story with “once upon a time...” In fact, this distinguished scribbler is a staunch believer that horror should begin that way. Good horror does not have to be original; it needs to be familiar enough to still surprise us.

The problem is not the simplicity of the premise, but the confusion of simplicity with emptiness. This direct-to-streaming production does not fail because it tells a conventional story; it fails because it seems to believe that recognizing the story is enough to tell it.

The reasons behind the supernatural activity are never explained, which could help amplify the threat were it not for the fact that its manifestations are so visually incomprehensible, thanks to the relentless and exhausting use of perceptual uncertainty. Bernini, who also serves as the editor, seems to ignore the fact that no device retains its impact when turned into a procedure; what might create unease the first time around becomes merely tedious when repeated ad nauseam.

The dark, shaky, deliberately confusing images, therefore, reveal less of an aesthetic choice than an inability on the director’s part to find anything genuinely interesting to show. The result is an awkward attempt to dress up a lack of ideas in an “art house” aesthetic, when it becomes obvious that the camera is not concealing what Bernini intends to show: it is exposing the fact that he has no idea what to show.

That aside, the rest of the film’s aesthetic construction is, by far, the best thing about the film. Benjamin Bradley-Gilbert’s cinematography is extremely beautiful, the color work is sophisticated, and the film look gives the production an admirable visual identity. Gabriel Bernini and his crew deserve credit for knowing how to emulate the analog aesthetic of horror’s Belle Époque so well.

The score is the second-best thing about Blood Barn. Jonathan Rado’s work does not rely solely on synthesizers, but also on a selection of music that seems to have escaped straight from the 1980s. The achievement also lies in the fact that it does not simply follow the action: some musical passages are strangely out of step with what is happening on screen, but precisely because of that, they acquire an ironic personality rather than functioning as mere dramatic punctuation. The sound design follows an equally careful logic, making use of noises, reverberations, and the acoustics of the location itself to give the space greater depth.

The screenplay is, in essence, as formulaic as one could expect from the premise: it introduces the rural property, establishes the family’s sinister past, and from there proceeds to proceeds to dole out supernatural manifestations and deaths without ever finding anything particularly interesting to do with them. And unlike some independent films that manage to seem larger than they actually are, Blood Barn gives the impression from the outset that there is almost nothing beneath the surface.

As for the cast, although some critics have described the performances as bad, they are actually quite compatible with what one expects from a B-movie. There are moments of greater artificiality, to be sure, but nothing so out of place with the film’s overall proposition or that can be held responsible for its problems. In fact, Chloe Cherry is in the film, but even her presence is not enough to save the story from the tedium of a narrative that seems increasingly determined to remain dull.

In the end, Blood Barn is not nearly as good a horror film as it is an exercise in reproducing that aesthetic. There is admirable cinematography, an entertaining score, and effective sound design. The film knows the past it wants to pay homage to, but has little idea what to do with it. Its director ends up reproducing the look above all else and forgets precisely what makes horror stories worth telling.
