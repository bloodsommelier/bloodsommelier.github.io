Prompt (2025)

Prompt (2025) knows the codes of ’80s horror, reproduces its mannerisms with remarkable diligence, and then considers the job done.

For much of Charles Band’s career, his fascination with the artificial took the form of dolls and pint-sized creatures. In recent years, however, his toys seem to have changed nature: AIMEE, Quadrant, and now Prompt reveal a producer and director increasingly interested in technologies capable of producing things that slip beyond their creators’ control. Prompt approaches the subject in a particularly frail manner: it is a film about technology that manages to be just as artificial as the thing it intends to examine. This time, Charles Band’s toy doesn’t bite, doesn’t kill, and doesn’t even seem particularly interested in working.

It all begins with a terrible credit sequence, as if someone had scoured the Internet’s worst stock-image library and used every embarrassing thing they could find.

The story is established immediately afterward: Taylor (Lilly Bell) works with AI-generated advertising and discovers a platform called Velvet Engine, capable of producing images and videos from text prompts. Naturally, being a story from the Full Moon universe, humanity would never waste its time creating an AI to solve climate change or put an end to wars. There are more important things to do: make pornography.

Taylor begins using the tool for this particularly libidinous purpose, but, to her horror, a mysterious individual keeps appearing in the images. The more she tries to figure out what is happening, the more the boundary between fantasy and reality seems to dissolve — or at least, that was the intention.

The idea is good enough for an episode of The Twilight Zone. But the film is more interested in Lilly Bell’s magnetic presence and in the possibility of generating computer images of lesbians.

Still, there are a few minutes left for philosophy: Taylor begins as the machine’s user. She gives it commands, creates images, and watches the results. Gradually, however, the relationship is reversed. The image no longer entirely obeys her will and begins to look back. In that sense, artificial intelligence is not exactly the monster of Prompt. The monster is the possibility that our fantasies have acquired enough autonomy to return our gaze. Someone send a copy of this to Nietzsche in hell.

It is a rather elegant idea for a film so utterly uninterested in developing it. The screenplay and direction make no attempt to add any depth to what the premise has to offer. The mystery never intensifies, and the presence of the stranger in the computer never becomes progressively more unsettling.

Prompt is not quite a minimalist film because it lacks the narrative elegance required to be one. Nor is it a claustrophobic techno-thriller, despite taking place almost entirely in what appears to be the church Charles Band has in Cleveland, Ohio — the same setting used in the equally sluggish Death Streamer (2023).

Instead, it is an extremely repetitive, tedious, exhausting film. A shame, because the central idea is genuinely interesting — as the final scene makes clear. Thus, Prompt ends up feeling closer to those interminably slow ’70s sexploitations than to the golden age of Full Moon.

Despite its short runtime, the film has remarkably little narrative density. The extremely simple plot revolves around only a handful of characters and remains almost entirely confined to the same spaces. With no subplots and no consistent escalation of the conflict, the film spends much of its time reiterating the same dynamic: Taylor creates a fantasy, the phenomenon manifests itself, and she tries to figure out what is happening. The result is a premise that would work perfectly well as a short film, but has been stretched to a length it simply cannot sustain.

On the other hand, Terrance Ryker’s cinematography is one of the few areas in which Prompt genuinely stands out. The film looks decidedly beautiful, with elegant colors and effective lighting that makes it look considerably more expensive than it actually was. The result is far superior to what most contemporary B-movies have been offering and makes Prompt one of the rare visually commendable examples of Full Moon’s recent output.

Lilly Bell is, without a doubt, the best thing in the film. She gives a performance that is quite well suited to the material, with the occasional exaggeration one expects from this kind of production. The actress carries the film almost entirely on her own, moving between eroticism and her character’s growing obsession, keeping the movie from becoming even more unbearably sluggish.

In the end, Prompt is a film that had a good idea and little else. The premise suggests paranoia, perversity, and an intriguing reflection on images that cease to obey their creator, but the film would rather repeat the same situations until even the artificial intelligence itself seems bored. Lilly Bell and the cinematography are enough to prevent a complete disaster, but not enough to save a feature that makes remarkably poor use of an idea that belongs in a short film.
