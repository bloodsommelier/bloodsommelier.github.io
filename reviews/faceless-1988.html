FILMS · EUROPEAN · 1988

Faceless (1988)

“Ce qu’il y a de plus profond chez l’homme, c’est la peau.”

A living face dissolves into the chemical impassivity of acid. A needle slowly pierces a rubbery eyeball. The topless prisoner convulses in excruciating agony. Behold the carnal poem — of sex and viscera — that Jess Franco writes in fake blood.

It is grotesque.
It is insane.
It is insanely grotesque.

When model Barbara Hallen (Caroline Munro) disappears into the bowels of Paris, her father, Terry Hallen (Telly Savalas, with the energy of a man who clearly wished he were in another movie), calls in detective Sam Morgan (Christopher Mitchum) to drag her back from limbo. The investigation inevitably leads to the clinic of the enigmatic Dr. Frank Flamand (Helmut Berger), where the doctor’s assistant and lover Nathalie (the ever-magnetic Brigitte Lahaie) delivers unsuspecting young women seduced by her fatal beauty. As witnesses are coldly dispatched and the clues slowly expose the horrifying conspiracy, the detective edges closer to the terrible truth lurking behind the walls.

Faceless is wrong in every detail — and right as a whole. The ultimate proof of the harmony of error: a piece of music that has absolutely nothing to do with anything happening onscreen plays incessantly, cradling the film in its wonderfully tacky, gloriously out-of-time pulp tone.

Although it flirts with gratuitous shock with a conspiratorial grin, Faceless is a dissonant amalgamation of the eighties and the sixties, held together by a deliberately imperfect harmony. The film’s surface — saturated with an aesthetic of violence already contaminated by the advent of home video— knows full well that it is inaugurating nothing, and is perfectly content to thumb its nose at “good taste” in favor of savage entertainment.

In the 1960s, black-and-white horror still invested in rupture, novelty, and transgression — the kind meant to carve real fissures into the viewer’s sensibilities. By 1988, innocence had already been deflowered. Faceless knows it has arrived late to the party, so it dresses for an orgy. And it is precisely this self-awareness that makes it so deeply eighties. Excess is no longer discovery; it is redundancy, the ghost of a violence that was once groundbreaking. And yet the film’s beating heart remains resolutely sixties. The mad scientist, surgery as an instrument of evil, identity conceived as something that can be ripped off and transplanted — these motifs belong to the mythology of 1960s European horror, not to the slasher that would come to dominate the multiplexes of the eighties. Franco resurrects this anatomical mythology not to modernize it, but to reenact it as a ghost. His interest was never in narrative mechanics, but in viscous atmospheres, recurring obsessions, bodies turned into landscapes.

Here lies the greater irony: a film about the exchange of faces that wears its own face as the genre’s belated mask. If the skin is what lies deepest within man, Franco films the surface as an abyss. The skin, ripped away and manipulated, becomes metaphor and matter at once. By exposing the artifice — the fake blood, the heightened theatricality — the filmmaker underscores that depth does not lie beyond the surface, but within it: in the texture of the image, the viscosity of blood, the fetishistic insistence upon the body. Faceless, then, is not a monument to horror, nor does it aspire to be one. It is instead a pulp epitaph to the European genre tradition, illuminated by the phosphor glow of an eighties CRT. Among ruins and echoes, Franco films as though groping through corpses in search of a pulse. And then, for brief moments, the skin breathes again — and how wonderfully fun that is!
